\section{Two-Digit Addition Circuit Reproduction in Qwen3-4B}

\citet{lindsey2025biology} report that Claude~3.5 Haiku does not solve
prompts of the form \promptinline{calc:~a + b =~} by a symbolic carry
algorithm.  The reported circuit instead uses sparse lookup features localised
by the ones digits of the two operands.  A feature activation might
encode various information, such as approximate operand magnitude,
$a \bmod 10 = d_1$ and $b \bmod 10 = d_2$ for some $d_1, d_2 \in {0, \ldots, 9}$, 
or ones digit of
$d_1+d_2$.  We use this result as the hypothesis for Qwen3-4B.  The
prior work gives the target pattern, but not every implementation detail needed
for this model.  We therefore state our Qwen procedure explicitly, using the
transcoder notation of Section~\ref{sec:interp-methods}.

\subsection{Operand and Residue Feature Scan}
\label{subsec:operand-plots}

For a prompt \promptinline{calc:~a + b =~}, let $T$ be the index of the
\promptinline{=} token.  For each feature $(\ell,k)$, we define the operand
matrix

\begin{equation}
  M_{\ell,k}[a,b]
    \;=\; a_{\ell,k,T}
          \left( \text{\promptinline{calc:~a + b =~}}\right),
    \qquad a,b \in \{0,\ldots,99\}.
  \label{eq:operand-matrix}
\end{equation}

The expectation is that sum-sensitive features produce diagonal patterns, 
single-operand features produce horizontal or vertical
bands, ones-digit features repeat with period ten, and joint lookup features form regular
grids of point clusters.

\paragraph{Token-position feature localisation.}
\citet{lindsey2025biology} report that operand-token features encode individual
digits, while delimiter-token features encode digit combinations.  We test this
separation in Qwen3-4B by applying Equation~\ref{eq:operand-matrix} at three positions
in the prompt \promptinline{calc:~36 + 59 =~}, namely at the ones digit of $a$ (position~$4$),
the ones digit of $b$ (position~$7$), and the \promptinline{=} token (position~$8$).
At each position, we seed the scan from the attribution graph for the focus prompt,
selecting the top~$15$ features by total edge weight and retaining one representative
per distinct ones digit.  Figure~\ref{fig:operand-pos-scan} shows the resulting matrices.

Features at positions $4$ and $7$ split between range detectors (vertical or horizontal
bands localised to the operand's tens range) and period-10 ones-digit detectors.
At the \promptinline{=} token, some sum-sensitive features form diagonal stripes of constant
$a+b$, while others show more diffuse activation.

\paragraph{Lookup feature identification.}
To identify specific lookup features for a digit pair $(d_1,d_2)$, we rank all
features by the fraction of their total activation mass concentrated at the
$200$ commutative cells satisfying $\{a \bmod 10,\, b \bmod 10\} = \{d_1,d_2\}$,

\begin{equation}
  s_{\ell,k}(d_1,d_2)
    \;=\;
    \frac{%
      \displaystyle\sum_{a,b}
        M_{\ell,k}[a,b]\;
        \mathbb{1}\!\left[
          \{a \bmod 10,\; b \bmod 10\} = \{d_1,d_2\}
        \right]
    }{%
      \displaystyle\sum_{a,b} M_{\ell,k}[a,b]
    }.
  \label{eq:lookup-score}
\end{equation}

To avoid redundancy, features with peak activation below $0.05$ are discarded.  
For the focus prompt
\promptinline{calc:~36 + 59 =~}, the top-ranked feature for the digit pair $(6,9)$
serves as the reference lookup feature.

\paragraph{Teacher-Forced Accuracy Analysis.}
To characterise the difficulty profile of two-digit addition in Qwen3-4B and what
structure the per-token confidence pattern reveals, we measure teacher-forced confidence at each answer position across
the full operand grid. If $\mathbf{y}^*(a,b)=(y^*_0,y^*_1,\ldots)$ is the correct answer
token sequence, then

\begin{equation}
  P_k(a,b)
  = P\!\left(
      y_k = y^*_k \;\middle|\;
      y_0 = y^*_0,\ldots,y_{k-1}=y^*_{k-1},\;
      \mathbf{x}(a,b)
    \right),
  \label{eq:teacher-forced}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{x}(a,b)$ is the prompt.  Teacher forcing removes uncertainty from
earlier generation errors and isolates the residual difficulty at position $k$. 
We show the results of this analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:positional-cascade}.

\begin{figure}[ht!]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Pages/Figures/positional_cascade.pdf}
  \caption{Teacher-forced confidence $P_k(a,b)$ over the two-digit addition grid at
    answer positions $k=0,1,2$. At $k=0$ (grid mean $\mu_0=0.233$), the low confidence is

    However, once the first token is forced, confidence rises to $\mu_1=0.943$ at $k=1$

\end{figure}

\paragraph{Entropy and carry-class analysis.}
Prediction entropy and carry class give the same negative evidence.  Entropy is
highest near carry boundaries and larger sums, but confidence does not decrease
monotonically with carry count.  The period-10 digit-pair grid is the stronger
axis.

\paragraph{Addition circuit interpretation.}
In Qwen3-4B, the addition circuit reproduces the main signature reported for
Claude~3.5 Haiku.  The model reads each operand at its digit position, then recombines the two
residues at the delimiter through joint-modular and sum-sensitive features.  The first answer token carries most of the difficulty,
and its confidence is organised by ones-digit pairs.  These results support a
lookup-like account of this scanned \promptinline{calc:} format and two-digit
grid.  The evidence does not support a sequential carry-propagation account for
this specific setting.

\section{Multilingual Antonym Circuit Reproduction in Qwen3-4B}

\citet{lindsey2025biology} report that antonym completion in Claude~3.5 Haiku
involves three separable circuit components: features encoding the semantic relation
(the antonym operation), features encoding the concept being negated (the input word),
and features related to the output language.
We test whether this decomposition holds in Qwen3-4B by independently suppressing
and injecting each component using the source-exclusive and target-exclusive
feature sets of the transcoder.
If operation and operand features are language-neutral, interventions identified from
English prompts should transfer to French and Chinese outputs.

\subsection{Contrastive Feature Set Construction}
\label{subsec:exclusive-features}

Each experiment runs the model on prompts that differ in only one
component (e.g. an antonym prompt versus a synonym prompt for the
operation experiment, or the same antonym prompt in English versus French for the
language experiment).  We refer to these as the source condition and target condition.
At layer $\ell$, let $a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{src}}$ and
$a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{tgt}}$ be the activation of transcoder feature $k$ under
the two conditions.  Among the top-$K$ active features, we define
source-exclusive and target-exclusive sets as

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{S}^\ell
    = \left\{k \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{src}}^\ell
        \;\middle|\; a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{tgt}} < \rho\,a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{src}}\right\},
  \qquad
  \mathcal{I}^\ell
    = \left\{k \in \mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{tgt}}^\ell
        \;\middle|\; a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{src}} < \rho\,a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{tgt}}\right\}.
  \label{eq:exclusive-sets}
\end{equation}

We fix the threshold at $\rho=0.5$ and leave a feature unchanged when it is
active at comparable strength in both conditions. Following \citet{lindsey2025biology}, we assign each circuit component a layer
range by dividing the 36-layer network into thirds: operation features are sought
in the middle third layers $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}} = \{12,\ldots,23\}$, operand features in the early third layers
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{oper}} = \{0,\ldots,11\}$, and output-language features in the first fifth layers $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lang}} = \{0,\ldots,6\}$. For the operation
and language interventions, we use the final prompt token as the anchor position; for the operand intervention, we use
the positions at which the two prompts first diverge (the operand word tokens).

\paragraph{Feature Suppression and Injection Policy.}

At these layers and positions, we project the pre-MLP hidden state
$\mathbf{h}^{\ell,p}$ through the transcoder encoder,

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\ell,p}
    = \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(
        \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{enc}}^{(\ell)}\mathbf{h}^{\ell,p}
        + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}^{(\ell)}
      \right).
  \label{eq:reenc}
\end{equation}

We ablate source-exclusive activations and inject target-exclusive activations

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{z}^{\ell,p}_k \leftarrow 0,
  \quad k \in \mathcal{S}^\ell;
  \qquad
  \tilde{z}^{\ell,p}_k \leftarrow \tilde{z}^{\ell,p}_k + \gamma v_k,
  \quad k \in \mathcal{I}^\ell,
  \label{eq:suppress-inject}
\end{equation}

where $v_k=a_{\ell,k}^{\mathrm{tgt}}$ and $\gamma \in \mathbb{R}$.  
We decode the modified feature vector
into the residual space,

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mathbf{o}}^{\ell,p}
    = \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{(\ell)}\tilde{\mathbf{z}}^{\ell,p}
      + \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{dec}}^{(\ell)},
  \label{eq:decode-multi}
\end{equation}

then replace the standard MLP output at that position.  We monitor the next-token
probability

\begin{equation}
  \pi_t(\gamma)
    = \bigl[\mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{logits}_T)\bigr]_t.
  \label{eq:pi-gamma}
\end{equation}

A successful component edit lowers the source token and raises the target token
as $\gamma$ increases. The intersection of the two curves indicates a \textit{crossover}.

\subsection{Operation, Operand, and Language Interventions}
\label{subsec:multilingual-experiments}

\paragraph{Operation edit.}
We identify features using a source prompt that asks for the opposite of
``small'' and a target prompt that asks for a synonym of ``small''.  We select
features at the final token over $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{op}}$ and apply the edit
to English, French, and Chinese antonym prompts.  If operation features are
language neutral, the edit should move each language from an antonym toward a
synonym.

\paragraph{Operand edit.}
We identify features using a source prompt that asks for the opposite of
``small'' and a target prompt that asks for the opposite of ``hot''.  We select
features at the operand divergence position over $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{oper}}$.
If operand features are language neutral, the edit should move the answer from
the antonym of \textit{small} toward the antonym of \textit{hot} in each
language.

\paragraph{Output-language edit.}
We identify features from source and target prompts that ask the same antonym
question in different languages.  We select features at the final token over
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{lang}}$ for EN$\to$ZH, ZH$\to$FR, and FR$\to$EN.  If
language is a separable component, the edit should move probability into the target
language while preserving the antonym relation.

\subsection{Multilingual Intervention Outcomes}
\label{subsec:multilingual-results}
 
We use $K=15$ active features per layer and $\rho=0.5$.
Token probabilities are resolved in context: the target token is taken from
the prompt-continuation tokenisation rather than from the surface form in
isolation, as quotation marks and non-Latin scripts alter segmentation
(e.g.\ \textit{large} within an open quote ``large'' may receive a different token
identifier than \textit{large} tokenised alone).

and inject synonym features, especially for Chinese and French prompts.  
The weaker English target peak may be attributed to a broader output distribution.

For the operand intervention, the English-derived \textit{small-to-hot} edit
moves the answer toward the antonym of \textit{hot} in English, Chinese, and
French. The operand concept therefore transfers across the
three languages with little loss.

The language intervention does not indicate a stable language component.  
The ZH$\to$FR and FR$\to$EN edit produce no
transfer, while the EN$\to$ZH edit raises the Chinese answer token by a small amount,
 yet not enough to count as an output-language crossover.  
 Thus, based on our experiment in Qwen3-4B, 
the selected early-layer
features do not isolate output language.

\section{Reproduction Findings}
\label{sec:repro-conclusions}

For addition, Qwen3-4B recovers the digit-pair organisation reported for
Claude~3.5 Haiku.  The model reads the operands as separate decimal residues,
then combines these residues at the delimiter through sum related
features.

For multilingual antonyms, we show that operation and operand
edits transfer across English, French, and Chinese.  This suggests that Qwen3-4B
uses features for the semantic relation and for the operand concept that do not
depend on one language.  However, for the output-language experiment, the same edit can
either suppress the original answer without moving probability to the target-language
answer, or not have any effect.

Based on our findings, some circuits transfer across
models and languages, while others depend on tokenisation, layer range, or the exact
surface form of the task.
