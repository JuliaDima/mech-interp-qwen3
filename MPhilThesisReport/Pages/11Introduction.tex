\chapter{Introduction}

Understanding what large language models (LLMs) actually compute, rather than what they output,
is the central problem of mechanistic interpretability. A model that correctly solves arithmetic problems or chains
reasoning steps coherently need not be implementing anything resembling the algorithm a human would use.
Whether it does, where in the network that computation lives, and whether it can be manipulated predictably
are empirical questions that evaluation benchmarks cannot answer.

Anthropic's \emph{On the Biology of a Large Language Model}~\cite{lindsey2025biology} proposes
a concrete methodology for approaching these questions. Combining sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
with cross-layer transcoders (CLTs) and backward-pass attribution, it constructs dependency graphs
that trace information flow from input tokens through intermediate features to output logits.
The framework is specific enough to be reproduced and tested independently,
which is the starting point of this work.

We take \texttt{Qwen3-4B-Instruct} as our subject model and integer addition as our primary task.
Addition is a reasonable prototype due to having unambiguous ground truth,
and being structured enough that prior work has identified non-trivial internal
mechanisms~\cite{nanda2023progress, quirke2024understanding}, and performance degrades in interpretable ways
as digit magnitude increases. We first characterise that degradation quantitatively,
then ask what the attribution graph methodology reveals about the underlying computation.

Beyond reproducing the attribution graphs, we use linear probes to localise where
arithmetic-relevant information is encoded across layers, and we test whether
features identified by attribution are causally relevant through two intervention
methods: activation steering and soft prompt optimisation. These experiments
are complementary as probes establish correlation between layer representations
and algorithmic quantities, while interventions test whether manipulating those
representations changes model behaviour in the predicted direction.

\marginpar[Margin note.]{Margin par.} We also report a negative result.
We attempted to transfer addition circuits from a small transformer trained
from scratch to \texttt{Qwen3-4B} by learning an affine map between their
MLP activations and patching at a target layer. The transfer failed.
We argue this is informative: it suggests that independently trained models do not share activation geometry
at the granularity required for naive circuit transplantation, which bears on broader questions
about cross-model interpretability methods.
