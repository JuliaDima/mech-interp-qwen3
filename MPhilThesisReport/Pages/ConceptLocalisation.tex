\chapter{Concept Localisation via Contrastive Residual-Stream Analysis}
\label{sec:concept_localisation}

This chapter aims to analyse where and how arithmetic concepts are represented inside
Qwen3-4B. We focus on three modular arithmetic problems: 
addition (carry detection), number divisibility, and the residue class a number belongs to. 
These concepts require the model to compute an abstract relation over numerical
values, and the goal is to determine whether that computation is reflected in
the model's internal activations in a form that is geometrically localised,
causally relevant, and decomposable into interpretable components and circuits. 

The motivation for this kind of analysis is that accuracy on a task does not
distinguish between a model that has internalised the relevant mathematical
relation and one that has learned a surface correlation that happens to produce
correct outputs on the evaluation distribution. Probing whether a concept is
linearly encoded at some layer is beneficial for finding layers responsible for an increase in accuracy, 
but it is not sufficient, as direction may exist because the model is passively carrying a result computed
several layers earlier, or because the prompt template introduces a confound that
the probe is sensitive to. The aim here is to provide evidence that goes beyond
linear separability, by finding where the concept emerges, how it evolves as the model reads the prompt, 
and what specific model components are responsible for solving that problem. 

The methods in this chapter are applied to Qwen3-4B across the three arithmetic concepts listed
above, following and extending prior work on extracting and using activation-space
directions as causal representations in language
models~\citep{zou2025representationengineeringtopdownapproach,
panickssery2024steeringllama2contrastive,
meng2023locatingeditingfactualassociations,fear2025physics}.

\section{Contrastive Pair Construction}
\label{sec:concept_pairs}

Each concept is represented by a set of matched prompt pairs
\((x_i^+, x_i^-)\). The positive prompt contains the target property and the
negative prompt differs in the smallest controlled way that removes it. Prompt pairs
are matched to avoid two prompts differing in many incidental details, in which case the mean residual
stream difference would mostly recover those nuisance changes instead of the
concept. The contrastive design attempts to hold syntax, length, operand scale, and
answer format fixed while changing the scientific property of interest.
For each concept the prompt template is chosen so that the raw model achieves at
least 10\% first-token accuracy, establishing that the model engages with the task
rather than ignoring it. Since the first answer token must directly express the
result of the input problem, changes in its probability under targeted interventions
serve as a direct measure of causal impact.

\section{Layer-Wise Concept Deltas}
\label{sec:concept_delta}

Let \(h_{l,t}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) denote the residual-stream vector at layer
\(l\) and token position \(t\) for prompt \(x\). Rather than fixing a single \textit{anchor} (input token position)
for analysing concept emergence,
we scan a set of candidate positions \(\mathcal{T}\), as different positions may encode different 
aspects of the concept: a delimiter may act as a preparation stage at which the model begins composing an
answer, an operand position may hold a representation of the underlying computation
before it is propagated forward, and the final position aggregates that information
for prediction.

For each layer, the concept delta is the mean positive residual stream minus the
mean negative residual stream at the anchor:
\begin{equation}
\delta_l
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+)
-
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-)
=
\mathbb{E}[h_{l,t^*}(x^+)]-\mathbb{E}[h_{l,t^*}(x^-)].
\label{eq:concept_delta}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, if \(\Delta h_{i,l}=h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+)-h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-)\), then
\(\delta_l=N^{-1}\sum_i \Delta h_{i,l}\). 

The trajectory $(\delta_l)_{l=0}^{L-1}$ traces how the concept representation
emerges as the model processes the prompt. At the embedding layer, $\delta_0$
reflects the direct difference in token embeddings at the anchor position; for
prompts that differ only in concept-bearing tokens far from the anchor, this is
often near zero. As the network accumulates contributions from successive attention
and MLP blocks, $\delta_l$ grows and rotates until it reaches a maximum at some
depth and then either stabilises or decays as the representation is transformed for
downstream prediction. The top row of Figure~\ref{fig:cosim_patterns_abc} shows this trajectory
for three anchor examples in studied concepts.

Under the linear representation hypothesis~\citep{park2023linear}, \(\delta_l\)
approximates an axis along which the concept is encoded at depth \(l\), provided
that the contrast is well controlled and the concept is approximately linearly
separable. 

\section{Anchor Selection}
\label{sec:delta_metrics}

The main statistic for concept emergence is the \textbf{norm trajectory}
\begin{equation}
D_l = \lVert \delta_l \rVert_2.
\label{eq:delta_norm}
\end{equation}

Because the residual stream accumulates contributions from every preceding layer,
the raw norm $D_l$ carries a monotone growth bias as later layers tend to have larger
norms without contributing to the concept. To remove this trend, $D_l$
is normalised by the mean residual-stream activation norm at each layer,
\begin{equation}
\mu_l^{\mathrm{act}} = \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\lVert h_{l,t^*}(x)\rVert_2\right],
\label{eq:mean_act_norm}
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken over both positive and negative examples. The
activation-normalised delta norm,
\begin{equation}
D_l^{\mathrm{act}} = \frac{D_l}{\mu_l^{\mathrm{act}} + \varepsilon},
\label{eq:act_norm_delta}
\end{equation}
is a dimensionless ratio that measures the fraction of the concept delta relative to the typical
residual-stream scale. To make trajectories comparable across anchors
with different absolute separations, the curve is further divided by its own peak,
giving the double-normalised trajectory
\begin{equation}
\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}
=
\frac{D_l^{\mathrm{act}}}{\max_j D_j^{\mathrm{act}} + \varepsilon}
\in [0,1].
\label{eq:double_norm}
\end{equation}

A peak in $\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$ identifies a layer where the concept delta is
disproportionately large relative to the ambient residual-stream scale.
Figure~\ref{fig:norm_comparison} contrasts the raw and double-normalised trajectories
for two anchor positions that exhibit qualitatively different regimes.

A sharpness score summarises how concentrated the delta norm is across layers. In
the implementation used for these experiments it is computed as the fraction of
total trajectory mass contained in the peak layer and its immediate neighbours.
Let $l^*=\arg\max_l D_l$. Then
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sharp}(t^*)
=
\frac{\sum_{l=\max(0,l^*-1)}^{\min(L-1,l^*+1)} D_l}
     {\sum_{l=0}^{L-1} D_l + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:sharpness}
\end{equation}
High sharpness indicates that much of the concept separation is concentrated in a
small processing window. Low sharpness, however, indicates either diffuse
representation, weak separation, or consistent separation from early to late layers. We aim to distinguish 
these behaviours by integrating multiple metrics.

The \textbf{inter-layer cosine similarity matrix} (see bottom row of Figure \ref{fig:cosim_patterns}) measures whether the delta direction is
stable across layers:
\begin{equation}
C_{l,m}
=
\frac{\delta_l \cdot \delta_m}
       {\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_m\rVert_2 + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:layer_cosine}
\end{equation}

Multiple qualitatively distinct patterns arise in practice, for which we provide the following
interpretation. A \emph{uniform high-similarity block} ($C_{l,m}\approx 1$ for all
$l,m$) (Figure \ref{fig:cosim_patterns}(a)) 
means the concept direction is geometrically stable throughout the entire
network at this anchor since the same axis is maintained across all processing stages.
A \emph{multi-block structure} (Figure \ref{fig:cosim_patterns}(b), 
showing high cosine within each of several contiguous layer
ranges but low or negative between them) means the concept direction undergoes a
sharp rotation at some transition layer $l^*$.  A \emph{diffuse
diagonal} (Figure \ref{fig:cosim_patterns}(c), showing gradual decay of $C_{l,m}$ as $|l-m|$ increases without a sharp block
boundary) indicates that the concept direction rotates continuously across layers,
with no concentrated processing stage. These three patterns are complementary to the
norm trajectory: a uniform block may have a smooth norm curve despite encoding the
concept at every layer, while a two-block structure typically shows a norm transition
near $l^*$. Figure~\ref{fig:cosim_patterns} illustrates each pattern with a concrete
example.

\citet{panickssery2024steeringllama2contrastive} report inter-layer cosine similarity
for steering vectors extracted from contrastive pairs in Llama~2, finding that
similarity decays smoothly with layer distance and that the rate of decay is slower
in the latter half of the model. They interpret this as evidence of concept-direction
convergence as once the relevant high-level information has been extracted, the
direction stabilises and changes more slowly. The heatmaps they show correspond to
what we term the diffuse diagonal regime. The residue class pattern in
Figure~\ref{fig:cosim_patterns}(c) is of the same character given that its cosine similarity decays
smoothly away from the diagonal with no sharp block boundary, and the concept
direction rotates continuously without converging to a stable axis. The uniform block and two-block patterns we observe for carry and GCD are
not discussed in that work, reflecting that the concept emergence at different acnchors
 can produce more structured representational trajectories than
the alignment behaviours they study.


To select which anchors are most important for isolating a concept, we construct a
 \textbf{non-monotonicity score}:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nm}(t^*)
=
\max_{l}
\min\!\left(
  c_l - \min_{j < l} c_j,\quad
  c_l - \min_{j > l} c_j
\right),
\label{eq:nonmonotonicity}
\end{equation}

where $c_l = \tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$. This quantity is the prominence of the most 
prominent local peak, and it equals the
smaller of how much the curve rises to reach layer $l$ from the left and how much it
falls away to the right. A monotone curve scores near zero while a sharp, isolated peak
scores near one.

\subsection{Null Permutation Baseline}
\label{sec:null_permutation}

A contrastive scan can produce apparent directions even when labels are
unrelated to the concept. The null test estimates this background level by permuting
positive and negative labels within their class and recomputing the delta
trajectory. Let \(\delta_l^{\pi}\) be the delta obtained under permutation \(\pi\). The
null distribution for a statistic \(q_l\) is
\begin{equation}
\left\{q_l(\delta^{\pi}) : \pi=1,\ldots,P\right\}.
\label{eq:null_distribution}
\end{equation}
The observed statistic is compared with the null mean and quantiles. A layer whose
observed score is outside the null range is less likely to reflect generic
prompt variability, thus becoming a reliable concept component (e.g., trajectories
shown as green in Figure \ref{fig:norm_comparison}).

However, a non-discriminatory anchor (one whose
observed statistic falls within the null band at every layer) does not imply that
no concept-relevant computation occurs at that position, especially when the corresponding 
inter-layer cosine similarity matrix is a uniform high-similarity block (see Figure \ref{}(a)). For example, in arithmetic tasks the
model internally computes intermediate quantities (digit sums, carry margins,
digit detectors) that may be prerequisites for the final answer without
separating concept-positive from concept-negative examples in a globally consistent
direction at the tested position. The null permutation test identifies positions
where the concept is represented \emph{discriminatively} across matched pairs, but it
does not exclude conceptually relevant computation at positions below the null.

\section{Causal Validation and Null Hypothesis Testing}
\label{sec:causal_validation}

\subsection{Activation Patching}
\label{sec:act_patching}

Activation patching tests causal sufficiency. For each pair \((x_i^+,x_i^-)\) and
layer \(l\), the positive residual vector at the anchor is inserted into the negative
forward pass:
\[
h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-) \leftarrow h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+).
\]
Let \(m(x)=\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}(x)-\ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(x)\) be the answer logit
margin at the final prediction position. The patching score is
\begin{equation}
S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[m\!\left(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-\right)-m(x_i^-)\right],
\label{eq:patch_score}
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-\) is the negative prompt with the layer-\(l\) anchor
activation patched from the positive prompt. A large positive score means that the
positive representation at that layer is sufficient to push the negative prompt
toward the positive answer.

\subsection{Gradient-Dot-Delta}
\label{sec:grad_dot_delta}

Gradient-dot-delta is a first-order approximation to patching. For each negative
prompt, the gradient of the margin with respect to the anchor residual stream is
computed, and then dotted with the mean concept delta:
\begin{equation}
S_l^{\mathrm{grad}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\nabla_{h_{l,t^*}}m(x_i^- )\cdot\delta_l.
\label{eq:grad_delta_score}
\end{equation}
A Taylor expansion gives
\begin{equation}
m(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-)
\approx
m(x_i^-)
+
\nabla_{h_{l,t^*}}m(x_i^-)
\cdot
\left(h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+) - h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-)\right).
\label{eq:patch_taylor}
\end{equation}
Averaging over pairs connects the patching score to the gradient-dot-delta score.
Agreement between the two curves suggests that the output is locally close to linear
in the relevant residual-stream direction. Disagreement suggests higher-order effects
or that the mean delta is not a reliable summary of the pairwise intervention.

 Figure~\ref{fig:causal_overlay_comparison} illustrates
this distinction with two contrasting carry anchors.

\section{Peak Layer Selection}
\label{sec:peak_selection}

Given the double-normalised norm trajectory, the null permutation baseline, and the
two causal scores, candidate peak layers are identified by combining four signals.
Let $e_l = \max(0, D_l^{\mathrm{act}} - \mathrm{null}_l)$ be the null-excess at
layer $l$. The combined score is
\begin{equation}
S_l
=
\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}
+
e_l
+
S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}
+
0.25\, S_l^{\mathrm{grad}},
\label{eq:combined_peak_score}
\end{equation}
where each component is normalised by its own peak before summation. The
gradient-dot-delta term is down-weighted because it shares gradient information with
the norm trajectory and is a first-order approximation to a generally nonlinear
effect. For anchors whose mean pairwise cosine across layers exceeds $0.5$ (
indicating a geometrically stable concept direction with low rotation) the
trajectory term $\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$ is excluded from the sum, since a
stable direction produces a smooth norm trajectory that does not distinguish
layer-specific processing. In that case only $e_l$, $S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}$, and
$S_l^{\mathrm{grad}}$ contribute.

Peaks are detected from $S_l$ using a prominence-constrained procedure with
prominence threshold $0.08$ and minimum inter-peak distance
$\max(3, \lfloor L/8 \rfloor)$ layers. 

\section{Feature-Space Interpretation}
\label{sec:feature_projection}

The residual-stream analysis identifies a dense concept direction, but does not
identify the individual transcoder features associated with it. To express
$\delta_l$ in feature space, we compare it with the encoder and decoder vectors of
each feature at layer $l$. The encoder vector describes the residual-stream
direction to which the feature is sensitive, whereas the decoder vector describes
the direction that the feature writes to the residual stream.

These comparisons assess whether a feature is sensitive to the distinction between
concept-positive and concept-negative states and whether its output is aligned with
that distinction. Equation~\ref{eq:concept_delta} defines $\delta_l$ as the
difference between the corresponding group expectations, with the positive
direction pointing from the mean concept-negative state to the mean
concept-positive state.

\paragraph{Decoder projection.}
Let $z_{l,f}(x)$ denote the activation of feature $f$ and
$w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ its decoder vector. The feature contributes
$z_{l,f}(x)w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ to the transcoder output. Because the decoder
vector is fixed across examples, linearity of expectation gives the difference in
mean contribution as
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
&\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{l,f}(x^+)w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right]
-
\mathbb{E}\!\left[z_{l,f}(x^-)w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right] \\
&\qquad =
\Delta z_{l,f}\,w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}},
\end{split}
\label{eq:dec_contrib_diff}
\end{equation}
where
$\Delta z_{l,f}=\mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^+)]-\mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^-)]$.
Its inner product with the concept direction is
\begin{equation}
\delta_l\cdot
\left(\Delta z_{l,f}w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right)
=
\Delta z_{l,f}
\left(\delta_l\cdot w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right).
\label{eq:dec_expected_projection}
\end{equation}
The first factor measures differential activation, while the second measures the
alignment of the feature's output with the concept direction. To compare this
alignment across features independently of vector norm, we use
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}
=
\frac{w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}} \cdot \delta_l}
     {\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2 + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:decoder_cosine}
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate decoder outputs aligned with the concept-positive direction,
while negative values indicate outputs aligned with the opposite direction.

\paragraph{Encoder projection.}
Let $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}$ and $b_{l,f}$ denote the encoder vector and bias. The
linear pre-activation is
\begin{equation}
u_{l,f}(x)
=
w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}\cdot h_{l,t^*}(x)+b_{l,f}.
\label{eq:pre_activation}
\end{equation}
Taking the difference between group expectations cancels the bias:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathbb{E}[u_{l,f}(x^+)]-\mathbb{E}[u_{l,f}(x^-)]
&=
w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}\cdot
\left(
\mathbb{E}[h_{l,t^*}(x^+)]
-
\mathbb{E}[h_{l,t^*}(x^-)]
\right) \\
&=
w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}\cdot\delta_l.
\end{split}
\label{eq:enc_mean_diff}
\end{equation}
Thus, the encoder projection is exactly the difference in mean linear
pre-activation between the two groups. Its normalised form is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}
=
\frac{w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}} \cdot \delta_l}
     {\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2 + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:encoder_cosine}
\end{equation}
Positive values indicate greater pre-activation (see Equation \ref{eq:encoder_cosine}) along the concept-positive
direction, while negative values indicate the reverse.

\paragraph{Combined score and feature pools.}
As described, the encoder cosine characterises feature sensitivity, whereas the decoder cosine
characterises the direction of the feature's output. We rank features by their sum,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{l,f}
=
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}
+
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}.
\label{eq:combined_cos}
\end{equation}
Same-sign terms reinforce one another, whereas opposing terms reduce the score.
The sign of $\sigma_{l,f}$ corresponds to overall alignment with concept presence and concept absence,
respectively. Both pools are ranked by $|\sigma_{l,f}|$, merged, and deduplicated.
Features that remain inactive are removed. Optionally, we also require a 
feature to occur in at least $5\%$ of the attribution graphs.

\section{Feature-Direction Interventions}
\label{sec:interventions}

To test both causality of our multi-metric analysis and impact of features resulted from
delta projections onto encoder and decoder vectors, we perform direct interventions given these
features. While transcoder based interventions are common in literature, we choose to perform 
modulation on the raw MLP output without invoking transcoder
reconstruction. The motivation for this approach is to preserve both the raw model circuit and
 first-token accuracy, as we observe consistent accuracy degradation 
 due to transcoder reconstruction errors.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Ablation removes a feature's contribution from the real MLP output. For feature $f$
with decoder direction $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ at layer $l$, a pre-hook computes
the transcoder activation $z_{l,f}(x) \geq 0$ by running the encoder on the MLP
input, and a post-hook then subtracts
\begin{equation}
y_l(x) \leftarrow y_l(x) - (1-\alpha)\, z_{l,f}(x)\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}},
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the suppression fraction ($\alpha=0$ is full
ablation; $\alpha=1$ is a no-op). The intervention is surgically restricted to the
nominated feature's contribution; no other part of the MLP output is modified.

\subsection{Injection}
\label{sec:injection}

Injection adds a fixed multiple of the decoder direction unconditionally at a
specified token position, regardless of whether feature $f$ fires naturally:
\begin{equation}
y_l(x) \leftarrow y_l(x) + \delta_f\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}.
\label{eq:injection}
\end{equation}
The injection magnitude $\delta_f$ is set to the mean natural activation
$\bar{a}_f^+ = \mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^+)]$ measured in the concept sweep. This
matches the injected signal to the feature's typical output when it fires naturally.
The operation does not require the encoder and introduces no reconstruction error.

Ablation and injection are complementary causal probes. Ablation removes the
feature's output from prompts where it fires and tests whether the model loses
concept-relevant behaviour; injection adds it to prompts where it does not fire and
tests whether the model gains that behaviour. Symmetric effects in both directions
establish bidirectional causal relevance.

\subsection{Carry in Multi-Digit Addition}
\label{sec:carry_results}

The carry concept for three-digit addition tests whether the sum of the units digits
of two operands exceeds nine. Positive examples satisfy $(A \bmod 10) + (B \bmod 10)
\geq 10$ and negative examples satisfy the strict inequality in the opposite
direction. The model achieves 100\% teacher-forced accuracy on all carry-positive
and carry-negative examples, indicating that carry computation is
fully solved at this difficulty level.

The concept direction peaks at the units digit of the second operand (raw position
9, model position 10 after the sink token) and at layers 4 and 5, with secondary
peaks at layers 19 and 22. The inter-layer cosine heatmap at this anchor shows a
two-block structure with a transition near layers 4--5 and a stable high-cosine
block thereafter, consistent with an early carry-detection stage followed by a
stable carry representation that persists to the final layers.

Feature projection via the combined score $\sigma_{l,f}$ identifies four concept-aligned
features: \texttt{L4\_F126502}, \texttt{L5\_F121933}, \texttt{L19\_F23877}, and
\texttt{L22\_F67294}
(dec\,+\,enc scores $0.84$, $0.63$, $0.72$, $0.62$ respectively).
The concept sweep confirms that these features fire on 23 of
99 positive pairs at the units-digit anchor, and on fewer than 5 negative pairs,
establishing high selectivity.
Figure~\ref{fig:carry_features} shows the mean activation of each feature on
carry-positive and carry-negative pairs, together with its decoder and encoder
alignment scores.

\subsubsection*{Ablation}

Joint ablation of all four features at full suppression ($\alpha=0$) reduces
positive-example accuracy from 100\% to 47.8\% on the 23 prompts where
at least one feature fires, while leaving negative-example accuracy unchanged.
Individual feature ablation has no measurable effect, indicating that the four
features are functionally redundant: each alone is sufficient, and all must be
removed simultaneously to degrade performance.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:carry_ablation}, the suppressed predictions
shift by approximately $-8$ to $-10$ relative to the baseline.
The consistent magnitude of these shifts is diagnostic of the features' computational
role: the offsets are close to $b_0$, the units digit of the second operand, which is
consistent with the carry features at the \texttt{ones\_b} anchor encoding the value of
$b_0$ as a contribution to an internal running partial sum.
When this contribution is removed, the model loses track of the digit-level value of $b$,
and the error propagates to the final predicted output.
The additional $\pm 1$ offset relative to $-b_0$ arises from carry propagation: ablating
the feature simultaneously suppresses the carry bit forwarded to the tens column, compounding
the digit error.
In two examples involving hundreds-digit carries (\texttt{948+904} and
\texttt{902+978}), ablation shifts the prediction by $-200$, suggesting that the
same features also gate higher-order carry propagation.

\subsubsection*{Injection}

Injecting the four decoder directions at the \texttt{ones\_b} anchor position with
fixed magnitude $\delta=5.0$ into 198 carry-negative prompts reduces prediction
accuracy from 100\% to 22.2\%, with 154 of 198 predictions changing.
Crucially, the injection is applied unconditionally to prompts where $a_0 + b_0 < 10$
and no carry should arise. Despite this, the model responds as though a carry has been
detected: Table~\ref{tab:carry_injection} shows representative examples where the
predicted sum increases by $+7$ to $+8$, shifting the units digit of the output
toward $9$ regardless of the true operand values.
The features therefore function as a carry-reminder signal in the model's
internal arithmetic: their decoder directions encode an instruction to increment the
running partial sum, and the model interprets their presence as evidence of a pending
carry, even when the true digit sum does not warrant one.
Bidirectional causal control is thereby established — ablation silences a real carry,
injection manufactures a spurious one — and both shift the predicted output by a
consistent magnitude, confirming that the decoder directions $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$
are a direct handle on the carry-increment computation.

An important observation is that the carry features fire on only 23 of 99 positive
pairs, yet the model achieves 100\% accuracy on all 99. The identified features
therefore constitute one carry-computation pathway among several, and the model
has additional redundant mechanisms not captured by the current feature projection.

\subsection{Fourier Patterns in Arithmetic Feature Grids}
\label{sec:fourier_patterns}

Each transcoder feature at a carry-relevant layer can be visualised as a function
on the ones-digit torus,
\[
f(a,b) = \text{mean activation of feature when ones}_{a} = a,\;\text{ones}_{b} = b,
\qquad a,b \in \{0,\ldots,9\}.
\]
The natural basis for such $10\times 10$ grids is the 2-D discrete Fourier system,
\begin{equation}
\exp\!\left(\tfrac{2\pi i}{10}(ua+vb)\right),
\qquad u,v\in\{0,\ldots,9\},
\label{eq:fourier_basis}
\end{equation}
so every grid decomposes into cosine modes $A\cos\!\bigl(\tfrac{2\pi}{10}(ua+vb)+\phi\bigr)$ where
$A=2|C_{u,v}|$ and $\phi=\arg C_{u,v}$.
The frequency indices carry direct arithmetic meaning: modes with $u=v=k$ depend on $a+b$
(iso-sum), modes with $u=-v=k$ depend on $b-a$ (iso-difference), modes with $u=0$
or $v=0$ depend on a single operand, and the $(5,5)$ mode captures parity $(-1)^{a+b}$.
A carry-margin detector $\mathbf{1}[a+b \geq 10]$ is localised on a diagonal and
requires many modes, while smooth periodic grids compress into one or two.

The top-$K$ reconstruction is
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(a,b)
=
\mu + \sum_{j=1}^{K}
A_j\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{10}(u_j a + v_j b)+\phi_j\right),
\label{eq:topk_fourier}
\end{equation}
with explained energy $E_K = \sum_{j=1}^{K}|C_j|^2 / \sum_j |C_j|^2$.
A feature with high $E_1$ is close to a single pure Fourier mode and can be read as
a smooth arithmetic coordinate; features requiring many modes to reach $E_K\geq 0.9$
are more localised threshold-like detectors.

\paragraph{Scan for Fourier-structured features.}
To systematically identify which transcoder features exhibit Fourier structure, the sweep
residuals at each layer are processed as follows. For every active feature $f$ at layer
$l$, the $10\times 10$ mean activation grid is computed and normalised to $[0,1]$.
A 2-D FFT is then applied and the AC spectral power is decomposed into iso-sum,
iso-difference, parity, row-only, column-only, and mixed components.
The \emph{structured Fourier energy} score $S_f = E_{\mathrm{diff}} + E_{\mathrm{sum}} + E_{\mathrm{parity}}$
collects the energy in the three classes with clear arithmetic interpretation.
Features with $S_f > 0.6$ are retained as Fourier-structured.

\paragraph{Localisation at the ones$_b$ anchor.}
Fourier-structured features in the carry sweep are found exclusively at the
\texttt{ones\_b} anchor (position~9), and not at other carry anchor positions.
This concentration is consistent with the causal evidence presented in
Section~\ref{sec:causal_patching}: the \texttt{ones\_b} anchor is the position where
the double-normalised delta norm peaks and where patching and grad$\cdot\delta$ are
jointly elevated, marking it as the principal locus of the carry computation.
The Fourier features cluster in layers 12--14 and layer 21, downstream of the
layers 4--5 peak identified by the concept direction analysis.

Figure~\ref{fig:fourier_selection} shows a selection of eight features from this scan,
each with its normalised activation grid and $K$-mode Fourier approximation.
The dominant structure across features is iso-sum (encoding $a+b$) or iso-difference
(encoding $b-a$), with $R^2 \geq 0.85$ in one to four modes.

\paragraph{Ablation.}
Joint ablation of all 60 Fourier-structured features identified at this anchor (including
all eight shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fourier_selection}) leaves model accuracy at 100\%
and changes the mean probability assigned to the correct answer by $+0.003$ --- a shift
indistinguishable from noise.
The features are thus present but not causally necessary for carry computation in this
model.

This dissociation suggests that the sparse autoencoder, while faithfully recovering
Fourier-structured directions from the MLP output space, has not isolated the
computational bottleneck. One plausible account is that the carry signal is distributed
across many features simultaneously, none individually sufficient, so suppressing a subset
has no discernible effect; the 100\% accuracy of the model before any ablation is itself
consistent with substantial redundancy in the carry pathway, as noted in
Section~\ref{sec:carry_results}.
Another possibility is that Fourier-structured features in the transcoder reflect a
representational geometry that the network has learned as part of pre-training on diverse
numerical text, without those features being on the critical path for the carry
computation specifically. Complementary Fourier-structured circuits may exist at other
layers or at token positions not covered by this sweep, and it cannot be ruled out that
compensatory mechanisms restore carry performance whenever the probed features are
suppressed. Settling whether the Fourier structure observed here is epiphenomenal or
part of a distributed arithmetic representation would require a more exhaustive
perturbation analysis spanning all layers and positions.

\subsection{Greatest Common Divisor}
\label{sec:gcd_results}

The GCD concept distinguishes prompts where $\gcd(A, B) = 7$ from those where
$\gcd(A, B) \neq 7$. Unlike carry, which is a local condition on individual digit
values, GCD membership depends on joint divisibility by a prime, a global property
of the operand pair. The analysis identifies several anchor positions that exceed the
null permutation band, indicating that GCD membership is discriminatively encoded.

\subsection{Residue Class Membership}
\label{sec:residue_results}

The residue class concept distinguishes prompts where $A \bmod 7 = r$ (for a fixed
residue $r$) from those where $A \bmod 7 \neq r$. In contrast to carry and GCD, the
residue class analysis reveals no anchor positions that consistently exceed the null
permutation band: the model does not appear to represent a clean binary concept
direction separating residue-$r$ from all other residues.

This null result is analytically meaningful. If the model's internal representation
of $A \bmod 7$ is organised as a Fourier-structured circular embedding, where all
seven residues correspond to distinct directions on a ring in activation space, then
the contrastive direction between residue $r$ and the union of all other residues
would be the difference between a single ring spoke and the mean of the remaining
six. Such a direction need not be stable across layers or geometrically concentrated
at any single anchor, because the ring distributes information across all residue
values simultaneously rather than partitioning them into a binary split. The absence
of a discriminatory null-exceeding anchor for residue class is therefore consistent
with, though not sufficient to confirm, a Fourier-structured internal representation
of modular arithmetic.
