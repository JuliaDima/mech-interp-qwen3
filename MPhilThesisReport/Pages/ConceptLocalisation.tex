\chapter{Concept Localisation via Contrastive Residual-Stream Analysis}
\label{sec:concept_localisation}

This chapter describes the contrastive localisation experiments used to identify whether an LLM encodes abstract 
scientific concepts as directions in the residual stream and where such concepts 
are localised inside Qwen3. The aim is not only
to find a layer at which a concept is linearly separable, but also to connect that
separation to sparse transcoder features and to causal effects on the model's
answer. The method therefore combines four views of analysing this localisation: residual-stream
deltas, feature and residual-stream alignment, activation patterns over structured datasets,
and intervention scores. This follows the broader use of activation-space directions as causal
controls over scientific concepts, including recent work on steering behaviours in LLMs~\citep{panickssery2024steeringllama2contrastive, 
zou2025representationengineeringtopdownapproach, meng2023locatingeditingfactualassociations} and scientific foundation
 models \citep{fear2025physics}.

\section{Contrastive Pair Construction}
\label{sec:concept_pairs}

Each concept is represented by a set of matched prompt pairs
\((x_i^+, x_i^-)\). The positive prompt contains the target property and the
negative prompt differs in the smallest controlled way that removes it. Prompt pairs
are matched to avoid two prompts differing in many incidental details, in which case the mean residual
stream difference would mostly recover those nuisance changes instead of the
concept. The contrastive design attempts to hold syntax, length, operand scale, and
answer format fixed while changing the scientific property of interest.
For each concept the prompt template is chosen so that the raw model achieves at
least 10\% accuracy, ensuring the model engages meaningfully with the task.

For the carry dataset, a positive example is an addition problem whose units digits
cross a carry boundary, while the negative example is chosen so that no carry is
required. For example, a pair may contrast
\[
\texttt{calc: 631+349=} \qquad \text{with} \qquad
\texttt{calc: 638+556=},
\]
where the exact operands are sampled from the dataset generator. For greatest common
divisor and residue-class datasets, the positive and negative examples are instead
grouped by modular or divisor-related labels. These datasets are useful because the
concept is a relation between numbers not tied to a single prompt token string.

\section{Layer-Wise Concept Deltas}
\label{sec:concept_delta}

Let \(h_{l,t}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) denote the residual-stream vector at layer
\(l\) and token position \(t\) for prompt \(x\). Each pair has an anchor position
\(t^*\), chosen to be the token position at which the relevant information is
available. In the carry experiments these anchors correspond to operand and answer
positions such as \texttt{rank5\_pos9}; in modular datasets they correspond to the
positions at which the relevant number or answer token is represented.

For each layer, the concept delta is the mean positive residual stream minus the
mean negative residual stream at the anchor:
\begin{equation}
\delta_l
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+)
-
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-).
\label{eq:concept_delta}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, if \(\Delta h_{i,l}=h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+)-h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-)\), then
\(\delta_l=N^{-1}\sum_i \Delta h_{i,l}\). This form makes clear that the delta is
not a single-example patch vector. It is an average direction over a distribution of
matched contrasts.

The trajectory $(\delta_l)_{l=0}^{L-1}$ traces how the concept representation
emerges as the model processes the prompt. At the embedding layer, $\delta_0$
reflects the direct difference in token embeddings at the anchor position; for
prompts that differ only in concept-bearing tokens far from the anchor, this is
often near zero. As the network accumulates contributions from successive attention
and MLP blocks, $\delta_l$ grows and rotates until it reaches a maximum at some
depth and then either stabilises or decays as the representation is transformed for
downstream prediction. Figure~\ref{fig:anchor_layer_grid} shows this trajectory
for the top-ranked carry anchors: the double-normalised norm row makes visible which
positions acquire a concept signal first and how that signal evolves through the
network.

Under the linear representation hypothesis~\citep{park2023linear}, \(\delta_l\)
approximates an axis along which the concept is encoded at depth \(l\), provided
that the contrast is well controlled and the concept is approximately linearly
separable. The delta should therefore be interpreted as a first-order summary of a
representation, not as proof that the model uses a single one-dimensional variable.
Several metrics are computed from \(\delta_l\) to test whether this summary is
geometrically stable, feature-aligned, and causally relevant.

\section{Anchor Selection}
\label{sec:delta_metrics}

The most direct statistic is the norm trajectory
\begin{equation}
D_l = \lVert \delta_l \rVert_2.
\label{eq:delta_norm}
\end{equation}
A peak in \(D_l\) means that positive and negative examples are most separated at
that layer for the chosen anchor. In practice, norm trajectories are used as an
initial localisation signal: early peaks often reflect lexical or template-specific
processing, while mid-to-late peaks are more likely to reflect an abstract variable
used by the computation.

Because different anchors can have different absolute scales, a normalised trajectory
is also plotted:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{D}_l = \frac{D_l}{\max_j D_j + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:delta_norm_normalised}
\end{equation}
The raw and normalised curves answer different questions. The raw curve shows the
actual geometric scale of separation. The normalised curve shows where an anchor's
own separation is concentrated, independent of its magnitude relative to other
anchors.

A sharpness score summarises how concentrated the delta norm is across layers. In
these experiments it is computed as a peak-to-average statistic,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{sharp}(t^*)
=
\frac{\max_l D_l}{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1} D_l + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:sharpness}
\end{equation}
High sharpness indicates that the concept appears in a narrow processing window.
Low sharpness, however, indicates either diffuse representation, weak separation, or consistent separation
from early to late layers. Anchors
are selected by combining peak norm, sharpness, and downstream causal scores. This
prevents the analysis from selecting an anchor merely because it has a large but
uninformative residual-stream scale.

The inter-layer cosine similarity matrix measures whether the delta direction is
stable across layers:
\begin{equation}
C_{l,m}
=
\frac{\delta_l \cdot \delta_m}
       {\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_m\rVert_2 + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:layer_cosine}
\end{equation}
Three qualitatively distinct patterns arise in practice and hold distinct
interpretations. A \emph{uniform high-similarity block} ($C_{l,m}\approx 1$ for all
$l,m$) means the concept direction is geometrically stable throughout the entire
network at this anchor since the same axis is maintained across all processing stages.
A \emph{multi-block structure} (high cosine within each of two contiguous layer
ranges but low or negative between them) means the concept direction undergoes a
sharp rotation at some transition layer $l^*$. The position performs two distinct
computations in sequence, with the late-layer direction occupying a different
subspace from the early-layer one. When the off-diagonal values are near zero the
two subspaces are orthogonal, indicating unrelated sequential computations; when the
off-diagonal values are negative the late-layer direction approximately negates the
early-layer one, a signature of sign-inverting processing at $l^*$. A \emph{diffuse
diagonal} (gradual decay of $C_{l,m}$ as $|l-m|$ increases without a sharp block
boundary) indicates that the concept direction rotates continuously across layers,
with no concentrated processing stage. These three patterns are complementary to the
norm trajectory: a uniform block may have a smooth norm curve despite encoding the
concept at every layer, while a two-block structure typically shows a norm transition
near $l^*$. Figure~\ref{fig:cosim_patterns} illustrates each pattern with a concrete
example.

\citet{panickssery2024steeringllama2contrastive} report inter-layer cosine similarity
for steering vectors extracted from contrastive pairs in Llama~2, finding that
similarity decays smoothly with layer distance and that the rate of decay is slower
in the latter half of the model. They interpret this as evidence of concept-direction
convergence: once the relevant high-level information has been extracted, the
direction stabilises and changes more slowly. The heatmaps they show correspond to
what we term the diffuse diagonal regime. The residue class pattern in
Figure~\ref{fig:cosim_patterns}(c) is of the same character: cosine similarity decays
smoothly away from the diagonal with no sharp block boundary, and the concept
direction rotates continuously without converging to a stable axis. Residue class
partitions integers into seven equidistant classes, a multi-way distinction with
inherently more degrees of freedom that does not reduce to a single stable binary
direction. The uniform block and two-block patterns we observe for carry and GCD are
not discussed in that work, reflecting that arithmetic concepts with sharper
decision boundaries can produce more structured representational trajectories than
the alignment behaviours they study.

Because the residual stream accumulates contributions from every preceding layer,
the raw norm $D_l$ carries a monotone growth bias: later layers tend to have larger
norms even if they contribute nothing new to the concept. To remove this trend, $D_l$
is normalised by the mean residual-stream activation norm at each layer,
\begin{equation}
\mu_l^{\mathrm{act}} = \mathbb{E}_{x}\!\left[\lVert h_{l,t^*}(x)\rVert_2\right],
\label{eq:mean_act_norm}
\end{equation}
where the expectation is taken over both positive and negative examples. The
activation-normalised delta norm,
\begin{equation}
D_l^{\mathrm{act}} = \frac{D_l}{\mu_l^{\mathrm{act}} + \varepsilon},
\label{eq:act_norm_delta}
\end{equation}
is a dimensionless ratio that measures the fraction of the concept delta relative to the typical
residual-stream scale. To make trajectories comparable across anchors
with different absolute separations, the curve is further divided by its own peak,
giving the double-normalised trajectory
\begin{equation}
\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}
=
\frac{D_l^{\mathrm{act}}}{\max_j D_j^{\mathrm{act}} + \varepsilon}
\in [0,1].
\label{eq:double_norm}
\end{equation}
A peak in $\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$ identifies a layer where the concept delta is
disproportionately large relative to the ambient residual-stream scale.

A scalar summary of peak concentration is used to rank anchor positions. Let
$c_l = \tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$. The non-monotonicity score is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{nm}(t^*)
=
\max_{l}
\min\!\left(
  c_l - \min_{j < l} c_j,\quad
  c_l - \min_{j > l} c_j
\right).
\label{eq:nonmonotonicity}
\end{equation}
This quantity is the prominence of the most prominent local peak: it equals the
smaller of how much the curve rises to reach layer $l$ from the left and how much it
falls away to the right. A monotone curve scores near zero; a sharp, isolated peak
scores near one. The top-$k$ anchors by this score are selected for detailed
analysis, since high non-monotonicity indicates that concept-specific computation is
concentrated in a narrow depth window, without being diffusely distributed across
the network.

\subsection{Null Permutation Baseline}
\label{sec:null_permutation}

A contrastive pipeline can produce apparent directions even when labels are
unrelated to the concept. The null test estimates this background level by permuting
positive and negative labels within the same template and recomputing the delta
metrics. Let \(\delta_l^{\pi}\) be the delta obtained under permutation \(\pi\). The
null distribution for a statistic \(q_l\), such as \(\lVert\delta_l\rVert\) or a
causal score, is
\begin{equation}
\left\{q_l(\delta^{\pi}) : \pi=1,\ldots,P\right\}.
\label{eq:null_distribution}
\end{equation}
The observed statistic is compared with the null mean and quantiles. A layer whose
observed score is well outside the null range is less likely to reflect generic
prompt variability. The null is computed per template so that template-specific
position structure is not mixed into the baseline.

A conceptually important point is that a non-discriminatory anchor --- one whose
observed statistic falls within the null band at every layer --- does not imply that
no concept-relevant computation occurs at that position. In arithmetic tasks the
model internally computes intermediate quantities (digit sums, carry margins,
divisibility conditions) that may be prerequisites for the final answer without
separating concept-positive from concept-negative examples in a globally consistent
direction at the tested position. The null permutation test identifies positions
where the concept is represented \emph{discriminatively} across matched pairs; it
does not rule out conceptually relevant computation at positions below the null.

\section{Feature-Space Interpretation}
\label{sec:feature_projection}

Residual-stream deltas are dense vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^d\). To connect them to
interpretable components, the pipeline projects each delta onto transcoder decoder
features. For layer \(l\), let \(W_l^{\mathrm{dec}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times F_l}\)
be the transcoder decoder matrix, and let \(w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\) be its
\(f\)-th column. This vector is the residual-stream effect contributed when feature
\(f\) is active.

The decoder cosine score is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}
=
\frac{w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}} \cdot \delta_l}
       {\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2+
       \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:decoder_cosine}
\end{equation}
A high positive value means that when the feature writes to the residual stream, it
writes in approximately the same direction as the contrastive concept delta. A high
negative value means that the feature writes in the opposite direction.

Analogously, the encoder direction $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}$ is the direction in
residual-stream space to which feature $f$'s detector is sensitive (row $f$ of
$W_l^{\mathrm{enc}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times F_l}$). The encoder cosine score is
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}
=
\frac{w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}} \cdot \delta_l}
     {\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}\rVert_2\,\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2 + \varepsilon}.
\label{eq:encoder_cosine}
\end{equation}
A high value here means the feature is tuned to respond to inputs aligned with the
concept direction, independent of how much it contributes to the output. In practical
terms, encoder directions are the directions used to detect feature presence, while
decoder directions are the directions used to write that feature back into the residual
stream. The combined score
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{l,f}
=
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}
+
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{enc}}
\label{eq:combined_cos}
\end{equation}
requires alignment through both the input sensitivity and the output contribution.
Features that score highly on both components are identified as concept-encoding
candidates and ranked by $\sigma_{l,f}$. Features with zero activation on all
dataset examples are excluded after the concept sweep regardless of their geometric
score, since they cannot causally contribute to the concept computation.

For a selected feature, the activation grid is formed by averaging feature activations
over arithmetic variables. In the carry case the grid is
\begin{equation}
A_f(a,b)
=
\mathbb{E}\left[z_{l,f}(x) \mid a(x) \bmod 10=a,\; b(x) \bmod 10=b\right],
\label{eq:activation_grid}
\end{equation}
where \(z_{l,f}(x)\) is the transcoder feature activation. The resulting
\(10\times10\) matrix can reveal whether a decoder-aligned feature is active on a
carry boundary, an iso-sum band, a parity pattern, or a more local arithmetic
condition. For intrinsic feature selection, the pipeline also records the fraction
of dataset examples on which a feature is active. Requiring nontrivial activity, for
example at least ten percent of examples, filters out features whose high cosine is
dominated by one or two isolated cells.

\subsection{Feature-Layer Duality and the Gram Matrix Connection}
\label{sec:gram_connection}

The decoder cosine scores $\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ compares a delta to a single feature direction, 
while the inter-layer similarity matrix $C_{l,m}$ compares each two deltas. 
Under the transcoder linear reconstruction, the two are directly connected.

The transcoder approximates the residual stream as a sparse sum of decoder directions:
\begin{equation}
h_{l,t^*}(x)
\approx
\sum_{f=1}^{F} z_{l,f}(x)\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}} + b_l^{\mathrm{dec}},
\label{eq:transcoder_approx}
\end{equation}
where $z_{l,f}(x) \geq 0$ is the transcoder activation for feature $f$ and
$b_l^{\mathrm{dec}}$ is the decoder bias. Taking the mean difference between
positive and negative examples and noting that the bias cancels, the concept delta
decomposes as
\begin{equation}
\delta_l
\approx
\sum_{f=1}^{F} \overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}},
\qquad
\overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}
= \mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^+)] - \mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^-)].
\label{eq:delta_decomp}
\end{equation}
Each scalar $\overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}$ is the mean differential activation of
feature $f$ at layer $l$: positive when the feature fires more on positive examples,
negative otherwise.

Consider now the inner product $\delta_l \cdot \delta_m$ that enters the
inter-layer cosine $C_{l,m}$. Substituting the decomposition gives
\begin{equation}
\delta_l \cdot \delta_m
\approx
\sum_{f,\,f'} \overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}\;\overline{\Delta z}_{m,f'}
\left(w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}} \cdot w_{m,f'}^{\mathrm{dec}}\right).
\label{eq:delta_inner_full}
\end{equation}
When decoder directions at the same layer are approximately orthogonal — a condition
that holds asymptotically for over-complete dictionaries and approximately for the
transcoder feature sets used here - the off-diagonal terms $(f \neq f')$ are small,
and the self inner-product simplifies to
\begin{equation}
\delta_l \cdot \delta_l
\approx
\sum_{f} \overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}^{2}\;\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\rVert_2^2.
\label{eq:delta_self_approx}
\end{equation}
Combining with the definition of the decoder cosine score
(Equation~\ref{eq:decoder_cosine}), one obtains
\begin{equation}
\overline{\Delta z}_{l,f}
\approx
\frac{\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\;\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2}
     {\lVert w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}\rVert_2}.
\label{eq:dz_from_cos}
\end{equation}
Substituting into $\delta_l \cdot \delta_m$ and dividing by
$\lVert\delta_l\rVert_2\lVert\delta_m\rVert_2$ yields
\begin{equation}
C_{l,m}
\approx
\sum_{f=1}^{F}
\operatorname{cos}_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}} \cdot \operatorname{cos}_{m,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}
=
\mathbf{p}_l \cdot \mathbf{p}_m,
\label{eq:gram_connection}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_l \in \mathbb{R}^F$ is the vector of decoder cosine scores at
layer $l$, indexed by feature. Under the transcoder approximation, the inter-layer
cosine matrix is therefore the Gram matrix of the per-layer feature-projection
profiles.

This identity has a direct interpretive consequence for reading the anchor summary
plots. A block of high values in $C_{l,m}$ indicates that layers $l$ and $m$ have
nearly identical profiles $\mathbf{p}_l \approx \mathbf{p}_m$: the same features
project onto the delta with approximately the same relative magnitudes. A sharp
transition in the heatmap corresponds to a layer at which the concept-aligned feature
set changes. The two panels therefore carry equivalent information expressed in
complementary coordinate systems: the inter-layer cosine matrix reveals when the
concept direction rotates, and the decoder cosine scatter reveals which features are
responsible for that rotation.

The approximation degrades in the presence of superposition, where decoder directions
are not orthogonal. In that regime the cross terms in
Equation~\eqref{eq:delta_inner_full} are non-zero, and $C_{l,m}$ receives
contributions from pairs of co-activated features. A systematic discrepancy between
the two views — blocks that appear in $C_{l,m}$ but not in the decoder scatter, or
vice versa — can therefore signal structured feature co-activation that lies outside
the sparse reconstruction picture.

\section{Fourier Patterns in Arithmetic Feature Grids}
\label{sec:fourier_patterns}

Many arithmetic feature visualisations are functions on a digit torus,
\[
a,b \in \{0,\ldots,9\}, \qquad f(a,b) \in \mathbb{R}.
\]
% Fourier analysis gives a compact way to describe these grids, and is especially relevant because
% pretrained language models have been shown to use Fourier-like numerical features when computing
% addition~\citep{zhou2024fourier}. 

The natural basis is
\begin{equation}
\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{10}(ua+vb)\right),
\qquad u,v\in\{0,\ldots,9\}.
\label{eq:fourier_basis}
\end{equation}
Every grid can be written as
\begin{equation}
f(a,b)
=
\sum_{u=0}^{9}\sum_{v=0}^{9}
C_{u,v}\exp\!\left(\frac{2\pi i}{10}(ua+vb)\right).
\label{eq:fourier_decomp}
\end{equation}
For real-valued grids it is usually clearer to report cosine modes,
\begin{equation}
A\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{10}(ua+vb)+\phi\right),
\label{eq:fourier_cosine}
\end{equation}
where \(A=2|C_{u,v}|\) and \(\phi=\arg C_{u,v}\) for a non-DC conjugate pair.

The frequency indices have direct arithmetic interpretations:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Frequency pattern & Interpretation \\
\midrule
\((k,k)\) & depends on \(a+b\) \\
\((-k,k)\) & depends on \(b-a\) \\
\((k,0)\) & depends only on \(a\) \\
\((0,k)\) & depends only on \(b\) \\
\((5,5)\) & parity, \((-1)^{a+b}\) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
For example,
\[
\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi(a+b)}{10}\right)
\]
is an iso-sum feature, while
\[
\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi(b-a)}{10}\right)
\]
is an iso-difference feature. Parity is a single mode because
\[
(-1)^{a+b}
=
\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi(5a+5b)}{10}\right).
\]

Not all arithmetic features are smooth. A carry-margin detector such as
\[
\mathbf{1}[a+b=10]
\]
is localised on a narrow diagonal boundary. It can still be represented in the
Fourier basis, but it requires many coefficients. This distinction is useful:
smooth sinusoidal grids indicate low-frequency arithmetic variables, while sharp
boundaries indicate logical or threshold-like detectors assembled from several
Fourier components.

In the plots, the top \(K\) Fourier approximation is
\begin{equation}
\hat{f}(a,b)
=
\mu + \sum_{j=1}^{K}
A_j\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{10}(u_j a + v_j b)+\phi_j\right).
\label{eq:topk_fourier}
\end{equation}
The explained Fourier energy is
\begin{equation}
E_K
=
\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K}|C_j|^2}{\sum_j |C_j|^2}.
\label{eq:fourier_energy}
\end{equation}
A high \(E_1\) means that the feature is close to a pure Fourier mode. A feature
requiring many modes is more localised and should be read as a detector rather than
as a single smooth arithmetic coordinate.

\section{Symbolic Fits to Feature Activations}
\label{sec:feature_fits}

For selected carry features, symbolic regression is used as a second description of
the activation grid. The input variables are engineered arithmetic quantities such
as \(a\), \(b\), \(a+b\), \(|a-b|\), \(ab\), \(\operatorname{mod}(a+b,10)\), and
carry-margin terms such as \(|a+b-10|\). The target is the normalised feature
activation \(A_f(a,b)\). A fitted expression is accepted only as an interpretive
summary of the grid, not as a claim that the model explicitly computes that formula.

The symbolic fit is evaluated with
\begin{equation}
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_{a,b}\left(A_f(a,b)-\hat{A}_f(a,b)\right)^2}
{\sum_{a,b}\left(A_f(a,b)-\bar{A}_f\right)^2}.
\label{eq:fits_2}
\end{equation}
Low-complexity expressions are preferred. If a feature is a simple carry boundary,
a short expression involving \(|a+b-10|\) is more informative than a high-complexity
formula with similar \(R^2\). The Fourier approximation and symbolic fit are therefore
complementary: Fourier describes periodic structure, while symbolic regression gives
a compact arithmetic predicate when one exists.

\section{Causal Validation and Null Hypothesis Testing}
\label{sec:causal_validation}

The preceding metrics are representational. They show that a contrastive direction
exists, but not that it is used to produce the answer. The pipeline therefore adds
causal and null-hypothesis checks.

\subsection{Activation Patching}
\label{sec:act_patching}

Activation patching tests causal sufficiency. For each pair \((x_i^+,x_i^-)\) and
layer \(l\), the positive residual vector at the anchor is inserted into the negative
forward pass:
\[
h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-) \leftarrow h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+).
\]
Let \(m(x)=\ell_{\mathrm{pos}}(x)-\ell_{\mathrm{neg}}(x)\) be the answer logit
margin at the final prediction position. The patching score is
\begin{equation}
S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left[m\!\left(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-\right)-m(x_i^-)\right],
\label{eq:patch_score}
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-\) is the negative prompt with the layer-\(l\) anchor
activation patched from the positive prompt. A large positive score means that the
positive representation at that layer is sufficient to push the negative prompt
toward the positive answer.

\subsection{Gradient-Dot-Delta}
\label{sec:grad_dot_delta}

Gradient-dot-delta is a first-order approximation to patching. For each negative
prompt, the gradient of the margin with respect to the anchor residual stream is
computed, and then dotted with the mean concept delta:
\begin{equation}
S_l^{\mathrm{grad}}
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\nabla_{h_{l,t^*}}m(x_i^- )\cdot\delta_l.
\label{eq:grad_delta_score}
\end{equation}
A Taylor expansion gives
\begin{equation}
m(\tilde{x}_{i,[l]}^-)
\approx
m(x_i^-)
+
\nabla_{h_{l,t^*}}m(x_i^-)
\cdot
\left(h_{l,t^*}(x_i^+) - h_{l,t^*}(x_i^-)\right).
\label{eq:patch_taylor}
\end{equation}
Averaging over pairs connects the patching score to the gradient-dot-delta score.
Agreement between the two curves suggests that the output is locally close to linear
in the relevant residual-stream direction. Disagreement suggests higher-order effects
or that the mean delta is not a faithful summary of the pairwise intervention.

The causal overlay plot places \(S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}\), \(S_l^{\mathrm{grad}}\),
and the delta-norm trajectory on the same layer axis. The useful case is not merely
a high delta norm, but a high delta norm that occurs near a positive causal score.
This alignment indicates that the representation is both separable and used by the
model's answer computation.

\section{Peak Layer Selection}
\label{sec:peak_selection}

Given the double-normalised norm trajectory, the null permutation baseline, and the
two causal scores, candidate peak layers are identified by combining four signals.
Let $e_l = \max(0, D_l^{\mathrm{act}} - \mathrm{null}_l)$ be the null-excess at
layer $l$. The combined score is
\begin{equation}
S_l
=
\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}
+
e_l
+
S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}
+
0.25\, S_l^{\mathrm{grad}},
\label{eq:combined_peak_score}
\end{equation}
where each component is normalised by its own peak before summation. The
gradient-dot-delta term is down-weighted because it shares gradient information with
the norm trajectory and is a first-order approximation to a generally nonlinear
effect. For anchors whose mean pairwise cosine across layers exceeds $0.5$ ---
indicating a geometrically stable concept direction with low rotation --- the
trajectory term $\tilde{D}_l^{\mathrm{act}}$ is excluded from the sum, since a
stable direction produces a smooth norm trajectory that does not distinguish
layer-specific processing. In that case only $e_l$, $S_l^{\mathrm{patch}}$, and
$S_l^{\mathrm{grad}}$ contribute.

Peaks are detected from $S_l$ using a prominence-constrained procedure with
prominence threshold $0.08$ and minimum inter-peak distance
$\max(3, \lfloor L/8 \rfloor)$ layers. The top-$k$ peaks by combined score are
returned as candidate intervention layers for the concept sweep and feature
modulation experiments.

\section{Feature-Direction Interventions}
\label{sec:interventions}

Geometric alignment and causal patching motivate direct interventions on identified
features. Both operations act on the raw MLP output without invoking transcoder
reconstruction, avoiding accuracy degradation from reconstruction error.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Ablation removes a feature's contribution from the real MLP output. For feature $f$
with decoder direction $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ at layer $l$, a pre-hook computes
the transcoder activation $z_{l,f}(x) \geq 0$ by running the encoder on the MLP
input, and a post-hook then subtracts
\begin{equation}
y_l(x) \leftarrow y_l(x) - (1-\alpha)\, z_{l,f}(x)\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}},
\label{eq:ablation}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \in [0,1]$ controls the suppression fraction ($\alpha=0$ is full
ablation; $\alpha=1$ is a no-op). The intervention is surgically restricted to the
nominated feature's contribution; no other part of the MLP output is modified.

\subsection{Injection}
\label{sec:injection}

Injection adds a fixed multiple of the decoder direction unconditionally at a
specified token position, regardless of whether feature $f$ fires naturally:
\begin{equation}
y_l(x) \leftarrow y_l(x) + \delta_f\, w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}.
\label{eq:injection}
\end{equation}
The injection magnitude $\delta_f$ is set to the mean natural activation
$\bar{a}_f^+ = \mathbb{E}[z_{l,f}(x^+)]$ measured in the concept sweep. This
matches the injected signal to the feature's typical output when it fires naturally.
The operation does not require the encoder and introduces no reconstruction error.

Ablation and injection are complementary causal probes. Ablation removes the
feature's output from prompts where it fires and tests whether the model loses
concept-relevant behaviour; injection adds it to prompts where it does not fire and
tests whether the model gains that behaviour. Symmetric effects in both directions
establish bidirectional causal relevance.

\section{Interpreting the Full Per-Anchor Summary}
\label{sec:anchor_summary}

For each selected anchor, the pipeline saves a vertically aligned summary over
layers. The panels share the same layer axis so that changes can be read together:
feature-decoder projection, raw and normalised delta norm, inter-layer cosine
similarity, null comparison, and causal overlay. This layout is designed to answer a
single practical question: at which layers is there a concept direction that is
large, stable, feature-aligned, above the null baseline, and causally relevant?

The interpretation is deliberately conservative. A high decoder cosine without
feature activation structure is only a direction match. A clean activation grid
without causal effect is only a representation. A causal score without template
robustness may be a surface-form artefact. The strongest evidence comes when these
signals agree at the same anchor and nearby layers.

Figure~\ref{fig:anchor_layer_grid} shows this summary for the six carry anchors with
the highest non-monotonicity scores (Equation~\ref{eq:nonmonotonicity}).
Each column is one anchor; the five rows correspond, from top
to bottom, to the decoder cosine scatter, the delta-norm trajectory (raw and
double-normalised), the inter-layer cosine heatmap, the null comparison, and the
causal overlay. The grid makes the structural regularities across anchors immediately
apparent: decoder-aligned features cluster near the same layers where the
double-normalised trajectory peaks and the causal overlay is high, consistent with
the Gram-matrix identity derived in
Section~\ref{sec:gram_connection}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:concept_results}

\subsection{Carry in Multi-Digit Addition}
\label{sec:carry_results}

The carry concept for three-digit addition tests whether the sum of the units digits
of two operands exceeds nine. Positive examples satisfy $(A \bmod 10) + (B \bmod 10)
\geq 10$ and negative examples satisfy the strict inequality in the opposite
direction. The model achieves 100\% teacher-forced accuracy on all carry-positive
and carry-negative examples, indicating that carry computation is
fully solved at this difficulty level.

The concept direction peaks at the units digit of the second operand (raw position
9, model position 10 after the sink token) and at layers 4 and 5, with secondary
peaks at layers 19 and 22. The inter-layer cosine heatmap at this anchor shows a
two-block structure with a transition near layers 4--5 and a stable high-cosine
block thereafter, consistent with an early carry-detection stage followed by a
stable carry representation that persists to the final layers.

Feature projection via the combined score $\sigma_{l,f}$ identifies four concept-aligned
features: \texttt{L4\_F126502}, \texttt{L5\_F121933}, \texttt{L19\_F23877}, and
\texttt{L22\_F67294}
(dec\,+\,enc scores $0.84$, $0.63$, $0.72$, $0.62$ respectively).
The concept sweep confirms that these features fire on 23 of
99 positive pairs at the units-digit anchor, and on fewer than 5 negative pairs,
establishing high selectivity.
Figure~\ref{fig:carry_features} shows the mean activation of each feature on
carry-positive and carry-negative pairs, together with its decoder and encoder
alignment scores.

\subsubsection*{Ablation}

Joint ablation of all four features at full suppression ($\alpha=0$) reduces
positive-example accuracy from 100\% to 47.8\% on the 23 prompts where
at least one feature fires, while leaving negative-example accuracy unchanged.
Individual feature ablation has no measurable effect, indicating that the four
features are functionally redundant: each alone is sufficient, and all must be
removed simultaneously to degrade performance.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:carry_ablation}, the suppressed predictions
shift by approximately $-8$ to $-10$ relative to the baseline, corresponding to the
model predicting a sum without the expected carry increment — the units digit of the
output rises by roughly 8 while the tens digit falls by one.
In two examples involving hundreds-digit carries (\texttt{948+904} and
\texttt{902+978}), ablation shifts the prediction by $-200$, suggesting that the
same features also gate higher-order carry propagation.

\subsubsection*{Injection}

Injecting the four decoder directions at the \texttt{ones\_b} anchor position with
fixed magnitude $\delta=5.0$ into 198 carry-negative prompts reduces prediction
accuracy from 100\% to 22.2\%, with 154 of 198 predictions changing.
Table~\ref{tab:carry_injection} shows representative examples: the predicted
sum increases by $+7$ to $+8$, corresponding to the units digit of the output
shifting from its correct value toward $9$ — the inverse of the ablation effect.
The symmetry confirms that the decoder directions $w_{l,f}^{\mathrm{dec}}$ for
these features causally encode the carry increment in the model's number
representation.

An important observation is that the carry features fire on only 23 of 99 positive
pairs, yet the model achieves 100\% accuracy on all 99. The identified features
therefore constitute one carry-computation pathway among several, and the model
has additional redundant mechanisms not captured by the current feature projection.

\subsection{Greatest Common Divisor}
\label{sec:gcd_results}

The GCD concept distinguishes prompts where $\gcd(A, B) = 7$ from those where
$\gcd(A, B) \neq 7$. Unlike carry, which is a local condition on individual digit
values, GCD membership depends on joint divisibility by a prime, a global property
of the operand pair. The analysis identifies several anchor positions that exceed the
null permutation band, indicating that GCD membership is discriminatively encoded.

\subsection{Residue Class Membership}
\label{sec:residue_results}

The residue class concept distinguishes prompts where $A \bmod 7 = r$ (for a fixed
residue $r$) from those where $A \bmod 7 \neq r$. In contrast to carry and GCD, the
residue class analysis reveals no anchor positions that consistently exceed the null
permutation band: the model does not appear to represent a clean binary concept
direction separating residue-$r$ from all other residues.

This null result is analytically meaningful. If the model's internal representation
of $A \bmod 7$ is organised as a Fourier-structured circular embedding, where all
seven residues correspond to distinct directions on a ring in activation space, then
the contrastive direction between residue $r$ and the union of all other residues
would be the difference between a single ring spoke and the mean of the remaining
six. Such a direction need not be stable across layers or geometrically concentrated
at any single anchor, because the ring distributes information across all residue
values simultaneously rather than partitioning them into a binary split. The absence
of a discriminatory null-exceeding anchor for residue class is therefore consistent
with, though not sufficient to confirm, a Fourier-structured internal representation
of modular arithmetic.
