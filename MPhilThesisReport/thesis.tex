\abstract{%
  Whether a language model that produces correct answers to an arbitrary problem has genuinely 
  internalised the underlying concept, or simply reproduces surface patterns from training, 
  is a question that evaluation metrics cannot resolve. This thesis addresses that question 
  through two complementary directions. The first reproduces Anthropic's circuit-tracing methodology 
  from \textit{On the Biology of a Large Language Model}, 
  analysing whether analogous interpretable circuits occur in an instruction-tuned language model, Qwen3-4B. 
  The second introduces a method for localising and describing geometries of abstract concepts within the residual stream
  and applies it to three structured arithmetic tasks of increasing representational complexity, 
  namely carry detection in single-step addition, greatest common divisor divisibility, 
  and residue class modulo problems. 
  Additionally, this method aims to generalise to non-arithmetic scientific domains, 
  provided concepts encoded through a set of contrastive prompt pairs.

  We aim to prove that robust and substantial analysis of concept emergence, rotation, 
  and propagation can arise from analysing contrastive pairs of prompts that differ only 
  in whether 
  a given concept holds, then computes the mean difference in residual-stream activations 
  across layers and token positions. These layerwise delta trajectories, normalised against a 
  null permutation baseline, expose where in the network the concept evolves. 
  The analysis then projects the delta vector onto the encoder and decoder directions of per-layer 
  transcoders, sparse autoencoders trained to reconstruct each MLP layer's output, to retrieve features
  contributing positively or negatively towards a concept.

  \dots
  
  The thesis also investigates soft prompt optimisation as a mechanism for targeted knowledge editing, 
  examining whether gradient-based modifications to the input embedding space can alter specific internal 
  representations without degrading broader model behaviour. Together, these results suggest that the 
  geometry of concept representation in large language models is not uniform, with some concepts 
  crystallising into low-rank directional structure susceptible to mechanistic analysis and others
   encoded in higher-dimensional arrangements that resist such decomposition.
}
