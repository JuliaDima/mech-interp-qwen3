\abstract{%
  Whether a language model that produces correct answers to an arbitrary problem genuinely 
  internalises its underlying computation, or simply reproduces surface patterns from training, 
  is a question that evaluation metrics cannot resolve. This thesis addresses this question 
  through two complementary directions. The first reproduces Anthropic's circuit-tracing methodology 
  from \textit{On the Biology of a Large Language Model}, 
  analysing whether analogous interpretable circuits occur in an instruction-tuned language model, Qwen3-4B. 
  The second introduces a method for localising and describing geometries of abstract concepts within the residual stream
  and applies it to three structured arithmetic tasks of increasing representational complexity, 
  namely carry detection in single-step addition, greatest common divisor divisibility, 
  and residue class modulo problems. 
  By its contrastive nature, this method generalises to other scientific domains, 
  provided concepts encoded through a set of contrastive prompt pairs.

  We show that concept emergence, propagation, and representational rotation can be studied through contrastive residual-stream geometry. For each concept, we compute layerwise \textit{delta} trajectories from matched prompt pairs and compare them against null permutation baselines to identify where the concept becomes geometrically and causally salient. We then project these dense concept directions into sparse autoencoder feature space, retrieving features that both activate contrastively and write along the concept direction. Their joint activation geometry defines a candidate concept manifold, while attribution-graph connections among them form a feature \textit{constellation} that describes how the model routes and transforms concept-relevant information during computation.
  
  In this thesis, we also study the optimisation of learned prefix embedding as a method for knowledge editing, 
  examining whether gradient-based modifications to the input embedding space can adapt specific internal representations without affecting broader model behaviour. We find that while this method generally improves model predictions, the learned embeddings are not interpretable. Instead of encoding identifiable concepts, they appear to encode, in a continuous form, an approximation of the quantity of information the model requires to produce the target output, without correspondence to structured semantic content.
}
